Train vs Bus vs Plane in Europe: Which Is Actually Cheaper?

Train vs Bus vs Plane in Europe: Which Is Actually Cheaper?

The honest answer: none of them wins every time. On a two-hour route, the train usually beats the plane once you count the airport. On a 900-kilometre route, the plane usually wins. The bus wins when you're flexible and the budget is tight. The only way to know for your specific trip is to compare door-to-door cost and time — which is rarely what the booking sites show you by default.

Here's how to do that comparison without spending an afternoon on it.

Why door-to-door is the only fair comparison

A ticket price is not a trip cost. Add the transfer to the station or airport, the buffer you have to build in, the bag fees, and the time you lose. Then compare.

Rough overheads to keep in mind:

  • Plane: 2–3 hours before departure plus transfers at both ends. On short routes, that overhead can double the journey.
  • Train: usually 15–30 minutes at the station, city centre to city centre, no bag fees in most cases.
  • Bus: similar to the train station overhead, often slower on the road, but sometimes the only direct option.

Two travellers with one suitcase each will find that the "cheap" fare with paid bags isn't cheap anymore. One backpack traveller might find the opposite.

When the train wins

The train is usually the better choice when:

  • the route is under about four hours by rail;
  • both cities have central stations;
  • you're travelling with luggage or with someone who doesn't love airports;
  • the train runs frequently, so a missed departure isn't a disaster;
  • you can book early enough to get a discounted fare.

Trains also tend to be more reliable in terms of arrival time, and you can work or read on board — which has a real value on a busy trip.

The catch: last-minute train fares can be expensive. The cheap seats are limited and sell out. If you know your dates, book early.

When the bus wins

The bus makes sense when:

  • the route is short, or there's no rail link;
  • the budget is the priority and the schedule is flexible;
  • you're going somewhere small, where the bus drops you closer than the train station would;
  • it's a night route and you can sleep on board (comfort varies a lot — read reviews).

Buses are often the slowest option, so don't book a tight connection on the other end. And check the boarding point: "city centre" sometimes means a stop on the edge of town.

When the plane wins

The plane wins on long routes and when the geography is against the rail network — think islands, mountains, or a route where the border crossing by land is slow.

It also wins when the flight is direct and the alternative involves two or three changes. A one-hour flight that replaces a ten-hour train journey with two transfers is usually worth it, even after the airport overhead.

But check: airport transfers, bag fees, and the arrival time. A cheap fare that lands at midnight can cost more than it saves.

Night trains: the "free hotel" trade

A night train can be excellent value: you sleep while you travel, and you don't pay for a hotel night. It also saves a day of sightseeing — you wake up in the next city.

The trade-offs are real: sleep quality depends on the cabin and the route, you need to book early for the good cabins, and a delayed night train can ruin the next morning's plan. Try one night before committing to a multi-night rail itinerary — it's the easiest way to learn whether you actually sleep on trains.

How to book train tickets for less

  • Book early. Discounted fares on long-distance trains are limited and disappear first.
  • Travel off-peak. Midweek and midday departures are usually cheaper than Friday evenings and Sunday afternoons.
  • Compare point-to-point with a pass. A rail pass is great for flexible multi-country trips; for two fixed journeys, separate tickets are often cheaper.
  • Check the exact station. Some cities have several, and the "cheap" fare might arrive far from your hotel.
  • Look at two one-way tickets. Sometimes that's cheaper than a return, especially across borders.

The same logic applies to buses: early booking and off-peak times.

Hidden costs to compare

  • bag fees (planes), seat reservations (trains), and booking fees;
  • airport or station transfers in both cities;
  • extra time at the airport;
  • an overnight stay if the cheap departure is at 6 a.m.;
  • food — airport prices are not city prices.

Add these to the ticket price before you decide. A slightly more expensive train that leaves at 9 a.m. from the city centre often beats a 6 a.m. flight, and the same logic works for buses: the cheap 5 a.m. departure is not really cheap if it adds a taxi and a lost morning.

A simple decision checklist

  1. Write down the door-to-door time for each option.
  2. Write down the total cost including bags and transfers.
  3. Decide how much your time is worth on this trip.
  4. Check the cancellation rules in case plans change.
  5. Book the option that wins on the combination — and don't feel bad about paying a little more for a much easier day.

FAQ

Is the train always more expensive than the bus?

No. On some routes, early-booked train fares are cheaper than bus tickets, especially for long distances. The bus usually wins on last-minute short routes with a tight budget.

Are night trains worth it?

For long routes with a comfortable cabin and an early booking, often yes: they save a hotel night and a travel day. For short routes, a regular daytime train is simpler and often cheaper.

Should I buy a rail pass?

Only if you're doing multiple journeys and need flexibility. For two or three fixed trips, separate point-to-point tickets are usually cheaper — compare both for your actual itinerary.

How early should I book trains in Europe?

As early as your plans are fixed. Discounted long-distance fares are limited and tend to sell out weeks or even months ahead, especially in peak season.
